ZONAL JETS

Jupiter's winds run in alternating bands — prograde at the equator near 150 m/s, reversing every few degrees of latitude. That measured profile drives the shear here, which is why filaments stretch sideways and tear at the belt edges instead of drifting as a uniform sheet.

DIFFERENTIAL ROTATION

The equator laps the mid-latitudes. System I (±10°) turns in 9h 50m 30s, System II outside that in 9h 55m 41s. Because each latitude is rotated by its own rate, the bands visibly slide past one another over time.

THE GREAT RED SPOT

An anticyclone at 22°S, wider than Earth, drifting slowly westward. Sample coordinates are rotated by an angle that falls off with distance from its centre, which is what produces the spiral rather than a painted oval.

OBLATENESS

It is not a sphere. Rapid rotation flattens Jupiter by 6.5% — the polar radius is 4 600 km shorter than the equatorial. The ray intersection uses the real ellipsoid, so the silhouette is correctly squashed.

WHAT THIS IS, AND WHAT IT ISN'T

Real: the zonal-wind profile, differential rotation rates, oblateness, the Great Red Spot's latitude and drift, limb darkening, and the terminator. Every pixel is a ray traced against an ellipsoid and shaded from those quantities.

Not real: this is not a Navier–Stokes solve. The clouds are domain-warped fractal noise advected by the wind profile — a simulation of how the atmosphere looks, not of the fluid itself. A true GCM would not run at 60fps in a browser tab. Worth saying plainly, because the difference matters if anyone asks.

Why it zooms: detail is generated, never sampled. Octave count rises as the field of view narrows, so new structure appears instead of a texture going soft. A texture-mapped Earth cannot do this at any file size.
